Play a color game every day and you'll get better at it. But is your *eyesight* improving, or just your familiarity with the game? Here's the honest, evidence-based answer.

## The short version

You can meaningfully improve at **discrimination tasks** — telling near-identical colors apart — through practice. What you're training is mostly your brain, not your eyes. And the gains are real but bounded.

## What perceptual learning actually is

Psychologists have studied **perceptual learning** for decades: with repeated practice, people get reliably better at fine sensory discriminations, whether that's radiologists spotting tumors, wine tasters parsing flavor, or you spotting a two-percent shift in lightness.

The improvement is genuine. But it comes with well-documented limits:

- **It's often specific.** Training on one task, one part of your visual field, or one kind of stimulus doesn't always transfer to others. Getting great at HUEMAN makes you great at HUEMAN first.
- **Your hardware sets a ceiling.** Practice sharpens how well your brain *uses* the signal from your cones. It doesn't add cones or change the fundamental optics of your eye.
- **Attention and strategy do a lot of the work.** A big chunk of "seeing better" is learning where and how to look — which is trainable and transfers more readily than raw sensitivity.

## So what improves when you play daily?

Probably a mix of:

1. **Better scanning strategy** — you learn to check the whole grid and let the outlier pop out.
2. **A lower practical threshold** — your brain gets better at extracting a faint difference from noise.
3. **Familiarity** — you know the ramp, so you don't panic on hard levels.

All three are real. Only the second is anything like "seeing better," and even that is your brain, not your retina.

## The important caveat

> HUEMAN is a **game, not a medical color vision test**, and it cannot diagnose color blindness or any eye condition. If your color vision seems to be changing, if you're struggling far more than people around you, or if you simply have questions about your eyes, see an **eye care professional** — an optometrist or ophthalmologist. A daily puzzle is not a substitute for a real exam.

## The takeaway

Can you train your color perception? Yes — within limits, and mostly by training your brain's attention and discrimination rather than your eyes' raw ability. The best part is that the training is fun: two minutes a day, one puzzle, the whole world playing the same one.
