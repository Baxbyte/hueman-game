Ask the internet how many colors humans can see and you'll get one confident answer: **about one million**. It's a decent estimate. It's also a bit of a fib.

## Where "a million" comes from

Human color vision runs on three types of cone cells in the retina, each most sensitive to roughly red, green, or blue light. Your brain reads the *ratio* of signals from those three cones and reconstructs a color from it.

Do the math on how finely we can discriminate along each of the three dimensions — hue, lightness, and saturation — and you land somewhere around a million distinguishable colors. That's the origin of the number: an estimate, multiplied out from three axes.

## Why the real answer is fuzzier

A million is a ballpark, not a measurement, for a few reasons:

- **"Distinguishable" depends on conditions.** Two colors you can tell apart side by side may look identical when separated — which is exactly the challenge HUEMAN exploits.
- **People vary.** Cone counts and ratios differ from person to person. Your million isn't precisely the same million as your friend's.
- **Context bends perception.** The same patch of color looks different depending on what surrounds it. Color isn't a fixed property you read off — it's something your brain *computes*.

## The people who might see more

Most humans are **trichromats** — three cone types. But a small number of people, likely some women, may be **tetrachromats**, carrying a fourth cone type. In theory that could multiply the number of distinguishable colors dramatically — into the tens of millions. In practice, confirmed functional tetrachromacy is rare and hard to prove, because our color language and our screens are all built for three cones.

## And the people who see fewer

Roughly 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some form of color vision deficiency, usually a shifted or missing cone type. It's not "black and white" — it's a different, often narrower slice of the same space.
